
Unified Sports Interface — Athlete Management System
Product Thinking Document · APM machine test for Khelo Tech · 13 October 2026
Summary
Every athlete in a federation is looked after by six to eight people: coach, physio, doctor, sports scientist, nutritionist and office staff. Each of them keeps their piece of the athlete in a different place. Decisions about training, injury and selection are made late and on half the picture, and nobody above the squad can see the whole programme.
The Athlete Management System (AMS) inside the Unified Sports Interface (USI) gives each athlete one shared record that every role works from. Work moves between people as tracked steps with a clear owner, and an AI layer spots problems early and suggests the next step.
Designed for: a national or state federation running many centres across many sports, such as federations working with Sports Authority of India (SAI) and Khelo India centres.
Demo story: Priya, a 16-year-old sprinter, followed from her first field test to an injury and her safe return to training.
Built deepest in the prototype: Medical & injury, Athlete onboarding & approval, Dashboard / Command Center, and Training & workload. The other five modules are present and connected, but lighter (section 7 explains why).
1. The problem
An athlete's story is split across seven places, so nobody sees the whole athlete in time to act.

Figure 1 · Where one athlete's information lives today · 7 places
Seven people each hold one piece of the athlete, in seven different places, and none of them sees the whole picture.
What goes wrong because of this:
What breaks
 | What it looks like on the ground
 | Nobody sees the whole athlete
 | The physio notices fatigue, the coach doesn't know and runs a heavy session, and the athlete gets injured.
 | Hand-offs get lost
 | Injury → physio → doctor → rehab → coach happens over calls and WhatsApp. Steps are skipped and athletes return too early or too late.
 | Problems are found too late
 | Overload and fatigue are noticed after the injury, not before it.
 | Leaders can't see across centres
 | A director waits for hand-made monthly reports to compare centres or spot rising injuries.
 | Talent gets missed
 | Test results sit in files, so a strong 14-year-old in one district is never compared with others nationally.
 | Athletes stop logging
 | Long forms mean few athletes fill them in, which means poor data and poor decisions.
 | Documents are hard to trust
 | Identity and age proof are hard to check at scale, which matters a lot in youth sport.

Problem statement. Each athlete is looked after by many specialists, and each holds one piece of that athlete's information in a different tool. So decisions are made late and on incomplete information, and leaders cannot see the system as a whole.

The buyer is not the daily user. The federation or performance director buys the system for its dashboards. Athletes, coaches and physios are the ones who enter information and act on it. If they don't, the director's dashboard is empty. So the design is built around three people:
Athlete: the morning check-in takes under 30 seconds on a phone.
Coach: the readiness view is worth opening before every session.
Physio: logging an injury is faster than the paper file.
3. How the product is organised
One athlete record sits at the centre. Six modules do the work in the order of an athlete's journey, three layers see across all of them, and everything sits on services USI already shares.

Figure 3 · Product map · 3 layers, 1 record, 6 modules, shared services
Read it top to bottom: the layers look, the record holds, the modules do, and USI provides the plumbing underneath.
The nine modules in the brief are not nine separate apps. Each of the six "doing" modules is one person's piece of the athlete, and the three "seeing" layers are how leaders and the AI look across the pieces.
The organisation ladder
Federation → State → Centre → Sport → Squad → Athlete
The ladder does three jobs at once:
Filters: what you are looking at ("U-17 sprints at the Pune centre").
Permissions: what you are allowed to see (a coach sees only their squads).
Roll-up: how squad numbers add up to the centre, the state and the nation.
Two design choices make it work in real life:
Home centre and temporary assignment. Priya trains at her home academy but may join a six-week national camp. The camp coach sees her training and readiness during the camp; her home centre keeps the full record.
Two ways to look. Leaders can look by organisation ("everything at the Pune centre") or by sport ("all sprinters in Maharashtra").

4. Key workflows
Four workflows carry the product. Every step has an owner and a status, so nothing waits unseen in someone's phone.
4.1 Athlete onboarding and approval
The record every other module depends on starts here.

Figure 4 · Onboarding and approval · 6 steps, 1 check, 1 loop back
A document problem loops back to the athlete with a reason instead of stalling silently; only a verified athlete reaches a coach.
Missing or unclear document: sent back to the athlete with the reason; the clock is visible to ops.
Possible duplicate athlete: the system shows the match and asks ops to merge or confirm.
Not eligible: rejected with a logged reason, so it can be reviewed later.
Under 18: a parent or guardian must give consent before medical information can be recorded.
4.2 The daily training loop
The heartbeat of the product. It runs every training day and is what keeps the dashboard live.

Figure 5 · The daily training loop · 8 steps
Athletes give two small inputs a day; the system does the maths, and the AI turns it into a warning the coach can act on with one tap.

 | 6:30 AM
 | Athletes answer five quick questions on their phone: sleep, soreness, mood, stress, energy
 | Works out a readiness score for each athlete

 | Weekly

Planning in three sizes. Coaches plan the season (the big picture, built around competition dates), then blocks of a few weeks with one focus (for example speed), then the week and each session. The session builder pulls drills from a shared exercise library and assigns them to a squad. Athletes marked Modified or Unavailable get an adjusted version automatically.
The one number that matters most. The system compares an athlete's load this week with their average over the last four weeks. A sudden jump well above their normal is the strongest early warning of injury. Sports scientists call this the acute-to-chronic workload ratio (ACWR); in the product it is shown simply as a "load jump" flag.
4.3 Injury to return-to-play
The brief's priority module, and the moment where the most people get involved.

Figure 6 · Injury to return-to-play · 7 steps, 2 decisions
Small issues take the light path; serious ones move through owned steps, and only a doctor's clearance sends the athlete back, with a limited load at first.
Interactive body map. The physio taps the injured area on a front or back body view, picks the side and severity, and the region changes colour. Past injuries show as faded marks, so repeat problems stand out.
Light path for small issues. Soreness or a niggle is flagged and watched without opening a full rehab case, so physios aren't buried in paperwork.
Return-to-play stages. Rehab moves through stages, for example walking, jogging, running, sprinting, then full training. Each stage needs the physio's sign-off.
Setback. A re-injury during rehab sends the athlete back a stage, with the reason logged.
Disagreement. If physio and coach disagree on readiness, the team doctor decides, and the decision is recorded.
Safe return. Fitness drops during time off, so jumping straight back to full training is when re-injury happens. After clearance, the system limits the athlete's load for the first weeks and raises it step by step.
4.4 Coach assignment and the coach's week
Once an athlete is approved, ops assigns a coach and a squad. The coach gets a notification, sees the athlete's profile and any open medical flags, and the athlete appears in that coach's next planned sessions.

6. Where AI helps
AI works inside each workflow, not in a separate chat window. It watches, warns, drafts and suggests; a person confirms anything that changes an athlete's plan or medical status.

Figure 8 · What the AI layer does · 6 jobs
The left side acts inside workflows (warnings, suggestions, form filling); the right side helps people understand (summaries, patterns, answers).
The AI Copilot is a panel available on every screen. It knows where you are and who you are: on Priya's profile, "how is she doing?" means Priya. Its answers come with actions, for example "3 sprinters are in the red today. Move them to the recovery session?", and one tap carries it out.
Three rules keep the AI trustworthy:
It always shows why. Every warning lists the numbers behind it (for example, sleep down three nights in a row and load up sharply).
It never changes medical status on its own. It drafts; the physio or doctor decides.
It compares each athlete with their own normal, not one fixed rule for everyone, because a heavy week for a 14-year-old is a light week for a senior athlete.

Trade-offs made on purpose:
Depth over breadth. Four complete workflows that click through from start to finish are worth more than nine half-built screens.
Realistic sample data. Indian centres, sports and athletes instead of empty templates, so every screen tells a believable story.
Staff on laptop, athletes on phone. Staff screens are built for desktop; the athlete check-in and effort rating are designed for a phone.
Left for later: offline use at grounds with poor network, regional languages, transfers between centres, and a full connection to competition management.

9. Assumptions and open questions
Assumptions made:
The first customer is a federation with many centres and many sports; examples use athletics.
USI already provides login, the athlete ID, the organisation ladder, permissions and notifications; the AMS uses them rather than rebuilding them.
Wearable data arrives through connections with device makers; the product does not build its own devices.
Medical and personal data follow India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, with guardian consent for athletes under 18.
